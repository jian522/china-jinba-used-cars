How RORO works

Roll-on/roll-off vessels load the vehicle by driving it aboard. You pay for the slot, not for a container. RORO is usually the cheapest option for a single driveable car and it is the standard service on high-volume routes such as China to West Africa and China to the Gulf. The trade-off is that you cannot add parts or personal effects inside the vehicle, and the sailing schedule follows the vessel's port rotation rather than your convenience.

How container shipping works

The vehicle is lashed inside a 20ft or 40ft container. This is the better choice when you ship several vehicles, when the unit is not driveable, or when you need to include spare parts. A 40ft high-cube container can often take two smaller vehicles with careful loading, which changes the per-unit economics completely.

Cost comparison that actually holds

There is no universal winner. RORO wins on single-unit simplicity. Container wins as soon as you share the box. Compare total cost per vehicle at destination, including terminal handling, not just the ocean freight line.

  • One driveable car, one buyer: RORO is normally cheaper.
  • Two or three cars for one consignee: a shared container usually beats multiple RORO slots.
  • Non-running or damaged unit: container or flat rack, not RORO.
  • Shipping spare parts with the car: container.

Damage and insurance

Both services carry risk. RORO risk is concentrated at loading and discharge; container risk is concentrated in lashing quality and in condensation on long voyages. In both cases, photograph the vehicle at loading, keep the survey report, and make sure marine insurance covers the full CIF value. A claim without loading photos is very hard to win.

What to confirm in the quotation

  • Service type and vessel or container reference
  • Port of loading and port of discharge
  • Whether freight is prepaid or collect
  • Free time at destination before storage charges begin
