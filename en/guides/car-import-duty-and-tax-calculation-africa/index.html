Home / Buying guides / How Import Duty Is Calculated on a Used Car

The taxable base is not the price you paid

Most customs authorities assess duty on a value they determine themselves, usually derived from CIF plus a reference value for the model and year. Paying $9,000 for a car does not guarantee that duty is calculated on $9,000. This is the single most common budgeting error importers make, and it is why a clearing agent's estimate before purchase is worth more than any online calculator.

The usual components

  • Import duty, often banded by engine size, model year or vehicle type
  • Value added tax or equivalent consumption tax
  • Levies, inspection fees and port charges
  • Age-based penalties in markets with vehicle age limits

Rates differ by country and are revised. Treat any percentage you see online, including on this page, as a prompt to verify rather than a quote.

Worked example of the method

Assume a CIF value of $10,000. If the applicable duty is 20% and VAT is 15% charged on the duty-inclusive value, the arithmetic is: duty $2,000, subtotal $12,000, VAT $1,800, total tax $3,800. Add clearing, terminal and transport costs and you have the landed cost. Change any single rate and the total moves by hundreds of dollars, which is why the rate check has to happen before purchase.

Getting a usable estimate

Give a licensed agent in the destination country the VIN, model year, engine size and CIF value, and ask for a written estimate that separates duty, VAT and fees. Compare that estimate against the vehicle price. If the landed cost is above what the vehicle sells for locally, the deal does not work regardless of how good the unit is.

Costs people forget

  • Port storage after the free period
  • Clearing agent commission
  • Inland transport from port to city
  • Registration and plates at destination
